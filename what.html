What is universal design?

It means designing so the widest range of people can use something from the start, with no special add-on or separate version.

Where it came from

Universal design started in architecture. Architect Ronald Mace, who used a wheelchair himself, popularized the term. In 1997 a team at the Center for Universal Design at NC State published seven principles that are still taught today.

The big idea: if a building has a ramp and no stairs, nobody needs a separate “accessible entrance”. Apply that to the web and there’s no separate “accessible version” of your site. There’s one site that works.

Three terms people mix up

Accessibility
Making sure people with disabilities can use a product, usually measured against standards like WCAG. It’s the checklist side.
Universal design
Designing for the widest range of people from the beginning, so one design works for most. It’s the mindset side.
Inclusive design
A method that involves people who are often left out, especially people with disabilities, in the design process itself. It’s the “nothing about us without us” side.

They overlap, and people use them loosely. Think of them as teammates, not rivals.

The seven principles, on the web

  1. Equitable use

    The same design works for everyone. Example: one checkout that works with a mouse, touch, keyboard, and screen reader.

  2. Flexibility in use

    People can choose how they interact. Example: zoomable and resizable text, light and dark themes, touch and keyboard controls.

  3. Simple and intuitive use

    You don’t need a manual. Example: plain language, consistent navigation, and labels that say what happens next.

  4. Perceptible information

    Key information reaches people through more than one sense. Example: captions and transcripts, alt text, strong contrast, and cues that don’t rely on color alone.

  5. Tolerance for error

    Mistakes are easy to fix. Example: undo, confirmation before deleting, and form errors that tell you how to fix them.

  6. Low physical effort

    Using it doesn’t take strain or precision. Example: big tap targets, no tiny drag handles, and autofill so people type less.

  7. Size and space for approach and use

    It works whatever your body, device, or setup. Example: layouts that adapt from a small phone to a wide monitor, with room around every control.

WCAG in one word: POUR

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the shared yardstick. They’re built on four ideas. Content has to be:

Perceivable

People can sense it. Text alternatives, captions, and enough contrast.

Operable

People can use it. Everything works by keyboard, with enough time and no seizure triggers.

Understandable

People can follow it. Readable text, predictable behavior, and helpful errors.

Robust

It works with many browsers and tools, including assistive technology.

WCAG has three levels: A, AA, and AAA. Most laws and policies aim for AA. The current version is WCAG 2.2, published in 2023.

Assistive tech worth knowing

Your HTML is what these tools read, so honest markup matters.

  • Screen readers (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver, TalkBack) read pages aloud. Users jump by headings, landmarks, and links.
  • Magnifiers and browser zoom enlarge the page, so layouts must reflow instead of breaking.
  • Voice control lets people say what they see on a button. Visible labels need to match the accessible name.
  • Keyboards and switch devices drive pages one control at a time, so focus order and visible focus matter.
  • Captions and transcripts make audio and video usable without sound.

Beyond disability, think about older adults, slow connections, small screens, glare, and people reading in a second language. Design for the edges and the middle gets better too.
