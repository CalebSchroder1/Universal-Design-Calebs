Everyone needs access. Some of us need it right now.

Universal design isn’t charity and it isn’t a niche. It’s how you make something that works when real life gets in the way, because real life always does.

Everyone is disabled sometimes

Access needs aren’t a switch that’s on for “those users” and off for everyone else. They’re a spectrum, and you move along it all the time. A need can be permanent, temporary, or situational.

Seeing

  • Permanent: blind, uses a screen reader.
  • Temporary: eyes dilated at the optometrist.
  • Situational: phone glare in bright sun.

Hearing

  • Permanent: deaf or hard of hearing.
  • Temporary: an ear infection.
  • Situational: a loud café, or a sleeping roommate at 1 a.m.

Using your hands

  • Permanent: one arm, or limited dexterity.
  • Temporary: a broken wrist.
  • Situational: carrying groceries or a toddler.

Design for the permanent case and the temporary and situational cases get solved too. One fix, a lot of people.

The numbers don’t lie

  • 1.3 billion people, about 1 in 6 worldwide, live with a significant disability, according to the World Health Organization.
  • 1 in 4 adults in the United States has a disability, according to the CDC.
  • 95.9% of the top million home pages had detectable WCAG 2 failures in the 2026 WebAIM Million. That went up from 94.8% the year before.

Here’s the part that should bug you: the failures are mostly basic. Low-contrast text showed up on 83.9% of those home pages. Missing image descriptions were on 53.1%. Missing form labels were on about half. These aren’t exotic edge cases. They’re the stuff this site’s How page fixes in an afternoon.

The curb-cut effect

Sidewalk ramps were won by disability rights activists. Now think about who uses them: people with strollers, rolling luggage, delivery carts, skateboards, and bikes. A change made for a few turned out to help almost everyone.

The web has its own curb cuts:

Captions
Built for deaf and hard of hearing viewers. Used by people in noisy places, people watching muted, and people learning a language.
Voice assistants
Grew out of speech technology that helped people who can’t easily type. Now they’re in every pocket.
Dark mode and high contrast
Essential for some people with low vision or light sensitivity. Loved by anyone reading at night.
Keyboard support
Required for people who can’t use a mouse. Also how power users fly through a form.
Clear layouts and plain language
Help people with cognitive disabilities. Also help anyone who’s tired, distracted, or on a bad connection.

If you grew up with captions on by default and dark mode on every app, you already live in the curb-cut effect. Universal design is how we keep building it on purpose.

It’s the next grassroots shift

Web standards, CSS Zen Garden, and HTML5 semantics each started with people deciding the web could be better and then showing how. Standards and semantics already did a lot of the groundwork: write good HTML and you’re most of the way to accessible.

Universal design asks us to finish the job. Treat access as part of design quality from the first sketch, not a patch after launch.

It’s practical too. Accessibility rules in many places, including the ADA in the US, Section 508, and the European Accessibility Act, point to WCAG as the yardstick. (That’s a heads-up, not legal advice.) And the habits overlap with good SEO and good performance: real headings, real text, fast pages.

Myths that hold teams back

“Accessibility is only for blind users.”

Disability is a wide spectrum: low vision, color blindness, deafness, motor disabilities, dyslexia, ADHD, anxiety, brain fog, a broken arm. Keyboard support, captions, contrast, and clear writing help all of them.

“It will make my design boring.”

Constraints make good design. CSS Zen Garden was one HTML file with hundreds of looks. This site has chunky outlines, hard shadows, and a purple palette, and every text color passes contrast. See the Meta page for the receipts.

“It’s too expensive.”

It’s cheapest at the start. Fixing contrast in a design system, or using a real button instead of a div, costs almost nothing on day one and a lot after launch. Remember that most failures are the basic ones.

“An overlay plugin will fix it.”

A widget can’t repair markup that’s wrong underneath. Many accessibility professionals and disabled users warn that overlays often get in the way. The dependable fix is in your HTML and CSS.

“We’ll fix it later.”

Later means rebuilding components, redoing designs, and retraining the team. Building it in means none of that. Start with the next thing you ship.
